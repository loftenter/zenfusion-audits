Is Access to Clean Drinking Water a Constitutional Right?

A federal appeals court says no. Six state constitutions say yes. The United Nations says yes. This is the story of Sterling v. Jackson, the lead-laced water behind it, and the fractured legal landscape that decides whether the water from your tap is a right or a privilege.

01The question in one paragraph

On September 4, 2026, the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit sat en banc and ruled, 10 to 5, that the U.S. Constitution does not guarantee any individual right to clean drinking water[1][2]. The case, Sterling v. City of Jackson, was brought by residents of Jackson, Mississippi who were knowingly supplied lead-contaminated municipal water for years[5]. The majority, per Judge Kurt Engelhardt, held that the deprivations were "grievous" but did not "infringe upon any deeply rooted constitutional right"[4]. The decision sits at the center of a three-way fracture in American water law: a federal Constitution silent on water, a growing roster of state constitutions that explicitly protect it, and an international community that has recognized clean water as a human right since 2010[13][14]. This article traces each strand of that fracture — the facts of Sterling, the statutory scaffolding that failed Jackson, the state "Green Amendments" that may yet succeed where federal law did not, and the global consensus the United States has chosen not to join.

02Sterling v. Jackson: the case that forced the question

The plaintiffs — Priscilla Sterling, Raine Becker, Shawn Miller, and John Bennett — are residents of Jackson, Mississippi, who sued the city and state officials under 42 U.S.C. § 1983, alleging that government actors had violated their substantive due process rights by knowingly exposing them to lead-contaminated water[22][23]. Their factual record is, by any measure, damning.

Between 2010 and 2013, the City of Jackson distributed municipal water it knew to be lead-contaminated[5]. The Mississippi State Department of Health documented lead violations as early as 2015 — yet residents were not informed for roughly six months after the agency had confirmation in hand[4]. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency issued an Emergency Administrative Order in 2020, followed by an Administrative Compliance Order in July 2021, formally recording that Jackson's water system had failed to meet federal lead and copper standards[3]. Then, in August 2022, the Pearl River flooded and collapsed what remained of the system. Roughly 150,000 residents lost access to safe water for weeks. The National Guard was deployed to distribute bottled water. And — in a detail that haunts the public-health record — residents who boiled their water to kill microbes, as officials had instructed, inadvertently concentrated the lead, raising their exposure even further[19].

The deprivations suffered by the Plaintiffs, while grievous, do not infringe upon any deeply rooted constitutional right. — Judge Kurt Engelhardt, majority opinion, Sterling v. City of Jackson, 5th Cir. (Sept. 4, 2026)[4]

The lawsuit was filed in 2022, dismissed by the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of Mississippi in 2024, and then taken up by the Fifth Circuit en banc[6]. By the time the full court ruled, the question had hardened into a single, narrow inquiry under qualified immunity doctrine: was the right to be free from contaminated water "clearly established" at the time of the violation? The majority said no. The dissent, led by Judge James Dennis, argued that the right to bodily integrity — long recognized in due-process jurisprudence — necessarily includes the right not to be poisoned by one's own government[4][3].

The split is not academic. A 10–5 en banc ruling on a question of this magnitude is, by Fifth Circuit standards, a strong signal that the issue is headed for the Supreme Court — and that the circuit is divided enough that the Court is unlikely to treat it as settled[6][24].

The Sterling facts did not appear overnight. They are the visible portion of a decade-long institutional failure — a chain in which each link had a chance to break the next and did not. 04What the Fifth Circuit actually said

The majority opinion is careful — almost defensive — about separating its moral assessment from its legal one. Judge Engelhardt does not deny the severity of the harm. He denies that the harm maps onto a right the federal Constitution recognizes. The opinion proceeds in two steps.

First, it frames the question under qualified immunity, which shields government officials from civil liability unless the right at issue was "clearly established" in light of existing law[6]. This is a deliberately narrow lens: it does not ask whether the plaintiffs should have a right to clean water, only whether such a right was already so well-settled that every reasonable official in Jackson would have known they were violating it. The majority concluded the right was not clearly established[4].

Second, the opinion gestures at the substantive due process doctrine of Washington v. Glucksberg, which limits fundamental rights to those "deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition." Under that test, the court found no lineage — no case, no founding-era text, no long-standing common-law rule — establishing a constitutional entitlement to lead-free municipal water[4]. The dissent countered that the right to bodily integrity is itself deeply rooted, and that knowingly poisoning a captive population is the paradigm case of a bodily-integrity violation[3].

Why the framing matters

By deciding the case on clearly established grounds, the Fifth Circuit left the underlying constitutional question — whether a right to clean water exists — formally open. Future plaintiffs can still litigate the right itself; they simply cannot recover damages from officials who acted before that right was settled. This is a narrower ruling than it appears, and it is exactly the kind of narrowing that invites Supreme Court review[24].

05The federal patchwork: statutes, not rights

The reason the Fifth Circuit could find no "deeply rooted" constitutional right is that, at the federal level, clean water is governed by statute rather than by constitutional text. The Safe Drinking Water Act (SDWA), enacted in 1974 and amended repeatedly since, authorizes the EPA to set enforceable standards for contaminants in public water systems[10]. The Clean Water Act, originally the Federal Water Pollution Control Act of 1972, regulates discharges into surface waters — a related but distinct regime[11]. Neither creates an individually enforceable constitutional right; both create regulatory obligations enforceable primarily by the EPA itself, and only derivatively by citizens.

Within the SDWA framework, the Lead and Copper Rule (LCR), first promulgated in 1991, is the operative standard for the contaminant at issue in Sterling. It sets an EPA "action level" of 15 parts per billion (ppb) for lead in the 90th percentile of tap samples — a level that is a treatment trigger, not a health-based safe level. The EPA's Maximum Contaminant Level Goal (MCLG) for lead is zero, reflecting the scientific consensus that no concentration of lead in drinking water is safe[7]. The gap between the action level (15 ppb) and the health goal (zero) is, in practical terms, the gap Jackson's residents fell into.

The LCR has been revised twice in recent years. A 2021 "Revised Lead and Copper Rule" strengthened corrosion-control and testing requirements, and a 2024 "Lead and Copper Rule Improvements" package moved toward a 10-year replacement deadline for all lead service lines[7]. These are meaningful regulatory steps. But they remain statutory — they bind the regulator and the regulated utility, not the Constitution, and they do not create a remedy for individual residents whose bodies were damaged before the regulations caught up to them.

06State constitutions: where the right actually lives

If the federal Constitution is silent, the state constitutions are not. At least six U.S. states have adopted some form of constitutional environmental rights amendment — the so-called "Green Amendments" — that recognize a right to clean air, pure water, or a healthful environment[16]. Pennsylvania was first, with a 1971 amendment to Article I, Section 27: "The people have a right to clean air, pure water, and to the preservation of the natural, scenic, historic and esthetic values of the environment"[14]. Montana (1972), Massachusetts (1972), Hawaii (1978), and Illinois (1970) followed with their own variants. New York joined in 2021, with voters approving an amendment stating that "each person shall have a right to clean air and water, and a healthful environment"[15][17].

The crucial question — and the one most relevant to Sterling — is whether these amendments are self-executing and enforceable by individual citizens, or merely aspirational. Pennsylvania has produced the most developed body of case law. In Robinson Township v. Commonwealth (2013), the Pennsylvania Supreme Court held that Article I, Section 27 is a substantive limit on legislative power, not merely a policy statement, and struck down portions of a statewide oil and gas law that overridden municipal zoning[14]. In Pennsylvania Environmental Defense Foundation v. Commonwealth (2017), the same court reinforced the amendment's enforceability and the public trust duties it imposes on the state as trustee of natural resources[14]. Together, Robinson and PEDF made Pennsylvania's right to "pure water" judicially enforceable — a precedent other Green Amendment states are watching closely.

New York's amendment is newer and its case law is still emerging. Fresh Air for the East Side v. New York is widely cited as the first significant test of the amendment's reach, challenging the state's regulation of air emissions from a facility in a residential neighborhood[17]. California, while not amending its constitution, took a statutory path: Water Code section 106.3, enacted in 2013, declares that "every human being has the right to safe, clean, affordable, and accessible water adequate for human consumption, cooking, and sanitary purposes" — a right the state is required to consider in its water policy decisions[16]. Mississippi, notably, has neither a constitutional nor a statutory right of this kind — which is part of why the Sterling plaintiffs had nowhere to turn but federal court.

08The rest of the world disagrees

The Fifth Circuit's holding that no constitutional right to clean water is "deeply rooted" in American history places the United States outside a global consensus that has hardened over the last fifteen years. On July 28, 2010, the United Nations General Assembly adopted Resolution 64/292, explicitly recognizing "the right to safe and clean drinking water and sanitation as a human right that is essential for the full enjoyment of life and all human rights"[13][14]. The vote was 122 in favor, none against, and 41 abstentions — the United States among the abstainers[14]. The InterAction Council, a body of former heads of state, had earlier called for the recognition of a right to water as part of a renewed human-rights framework[20].

The UN Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights now treats the human right to water and sanitation as established international law, with independent reporting mechanisms and country-level reviews[13]. At least 17 national constitutions explicitly guarantee a right to water, and several more recognize it through judicial interpretation of broader rights to life, health, or a healthy environment[14]. South Africa's post-apartheid Constitution (1996) guarantees that "everyone has the right to have access to… sufficient… water," and its courts have enforced that right against municipal disconnections[14]. Uruguay amended its constitution in 2004 to explicitly prohibit the privatization of water services, treating access as a public good[14].

The United States is, by the measure of constitutional text, in the minority: a wealthy democracy whose foundational document says nothing about the water its citizens drink.

None of this binds a U.S. court — the Fifth Circuit correctly noted that international law does not, on its own, establish a right "deeply rooted" in American history for Glucksberg purposes[4]. But international practice is relevant in two softer ways. First, it supplies comparative evidence that a constitutional right to water is workable — the doomsday predictions of unmanageable litigation have not materialized in the jurisdictions that adopted one. Second, it shapes the political and reputational environment in which U.S. courts and legislatures operate. The longer the U.S. stands apart, the more conspicuous the standing-apart becomes.

10Why lead is the test case

Lead is the contaminant that makes the absence of a constitutional right most visible, because it is the contaminant for which there is no safe exposure. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention states unambiguously that there is no safe blood lead level for children[9]. The EPA's MCLG for lead in drinking water is zero[7]. The physiological effects are well-established and irreversible: brain and nervous-system damage, learning and behavior problems, lowered IQ, slowed growth, hearing problems, and anemia in children[21][8]. The developing brain is the principal target, and the damage is dose-dependent even at very low exposures — there is no threshold below which lead is harmless[21].

Drinking water is a meaningful fraction of total lead exposure. The EPA estimates that water can account for up to 20% of an average adult's lead exposure, and 40–60% of exposure for formula-fed infants whose sole nutrition is mixed with tap water[7]. In a city like Jackson, where the water itself is the contamination source and the official guidance — boiling — cannot remove lead and can concentrate it, the exposure pathway is direct, sustained, and disproportionately borne by the youngest residents[19].

This is why Sterling is not just a procedural case about qualified immunity. It is a case in which the factual record shows a government knowingly delivering a neurotoxin to children over years, a federal regulatory apparatus that documented the failure and issued orders but could not restore safe water, and a constitutional doctrine that, in the Fifth Circuit's reading, has nothing to say about any of it. The gap between the harm and the available remedy is the entire point.

11What residents can actually do

The legal landscape described above is, for now, hostile to a federal constitutional claim. That does not leave residents of contaminated systems without options — but the options are practical and political rather than constitutional, and they place the burden of protection on the household rather than the state.

  • Test your water. The only way to know whether lead is present at the tap — where it matters, not at the treatment plant — is to test. EPA-certified laboratories will analyze tap samples for lead; many municipal water systems are required to provide sample kits on request under the LCR[7][8]. Test results that exceed 15 ppb at the 90th-percentile level trigger regulatory action; results that exceed the MCLG of zero warrant household-level intervention regardless of the regulatory threshold.
  • Invest in home water filtration. Point-of-use filtration is the most reliable household-level intervention. Reverse osmosis systems, properly certified lead-removal filters (NSF/ANSI Standard 53 for lead reduction, Standard 58 for reverse osmosis), and certain activated-carbon block filters can reduce lead at the tap to below detectable levels. Filters must be maintained — an expired cartridge can itself become a source of contamination. Boiling does not remove lead and, as Jackson's residents learned, can concentrate it[8][19].
  • Support state-level Green Amendments. Because the federal Constitution is, for now, closed, the most promising legal route for recognizing a right to clean water runs through the states. The Pennsylvania model — a self-executing amendment enforced by courts — is the precedent advocates point to. New York's 2021 amendment and the emerging case law around it are the live experiment[15][16]. Activist coalitions in several states without such amendments are working to place them on the ballot; supporting those efforts is, at present, the most direct path to making clean water a legally enforceable right rather than a regulatory aspiration.
  • Use existing statutory remedies. The SDWA's citizen-suit provisions, state public-nuisance law, and the Lead and Copper Rule's enforcement mechanisms remain available even where constitutional claims fail. These remedies are slower, narrower, and often capped, but they are the ones the current legal regime actually provides[10].

12The path forward

The Sterling decision closes a door and identifies the next ones. The closed door is the federal due-process claim, at least in the Fifth Circuit and at least for damages under qualified immunity. The doors that remain open are the Supreme Court, which now has a clean vehicle to address the underlying right; the state constitutions, where Green Amendments are slowly building a body of enforceable water-rights law; and the political branches, which could at any time recognize a federal right by statute or amendment if the courts will not.

What Sterling makes inescapable is the cost of the current arrangement. A city knowingly delivered lead-contaminated water to its residents for years. A state health department sat on the knowledge. A federal regulator issued orders that did not restore safe water. A flood finished what neglect had started. And the constitutional doctrine that governs the relationship between an American citizen and an American government said, in the end, that none of this infringed a right "deeply rooted" enough to be recognized. The question is not whether clean water matters — everyone agrees it does. The question is whether the law will say so. Right now, in most of the United States, it does not.

The next move belongs to whichever institution — the Supreme Court, a state electorate, or Congress — decides to be the one that changes that. Until one of them does, the right to clean water in the United States is, in the precise sense the Fifth Circuit described, a right that exists everywhere except in the place a right is supposed to be.
