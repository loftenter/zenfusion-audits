1. The 1% Myth: What You Actually Use Water For

When most homeowners think about water treatment, they imagine drinking water — a glass at the kitchen sink, a pitcher in the fridge, water for coffee and tea. It makes sense: we're told to drink eight glasses a day, so the water we ingest seems like the obvious priority.

But here's what the numbers actually say. According to a fact sheet published by Kinetico and the Water Quality Association, only about 1 percent of the water entering a typical home is used for drinking.1 The remaining 99% — aptly called "working water" — flows through your showers, baths, washing machine, dishwasher, faucets where you brush your teeth, outdoor spigots, and toilet tanks.

That 99% touches your skin every morning in the shower. It runs through your clothes in the laundry. It circulates through your water heater, your dishwasher, and your coffee maker. And when you only treat the drinking tap, that 99% is still carrying everything your municipal utility adds — and fails to remove — into your home.

2. Three Exposure Routes Your Filter Isn't Blocking

This is where the gap in point-of-use (POU) protection becomes genuinely concerning. Drinking water ingestion is only one of three major routes by which waterborne contaminants enter your body — and for some contaminants, it may not even be the largest.

Dermal Absorption in the Shower

Your skin is your largest organ, and hot showers open your pores and thin the stratum corneum, dramatically increasing transdermal absorption.2 Municipal chlorine — added at levels between 1.0 and 4.0 parts per million as a disinfectant — is readily absorbed through skin. A study published in the American Journal of Public Health found that dermal absorption of chlorine during showering accounted for a substantial portion of total daily chlorine exposure, in some models exceeding the contribution from drinking water.3

Chloramine (chlorine + ammonia), which many municipal utilities now use because it is more stable than free chlorine, is even more problematic. It is lipophilic, meaning it dissolves into the skin's natural sebum layer and disrupts the acid mantle that protects against pathogens.4 And unlike free chlorine, chloramine does not evaporate at hot-water temperatures — you are exposed for the entire duration of your shower.

Inhalation of Volatile Compounds in Steam

Perhaps the most overlooked exposure route is inhalation. When hot water hits the shower floor, chlorine and chloramine react with organic matter to form trihalomethanes (THMs) — volatile compounds that include chloroform, a probable human carcinogen classified by the EPA.5 Research published in Environmental Health Perspectives by Weisel and Jo found that inhalation during a 10-minute shower can account for more THM exposure than drinking 2 litres of the same water every day.6

The lungs are far more efficient at absorbing volatile compounds into the bloodstream than the digestive system is at processing ingested ones. Every hot shower becomes a de facto chemical exposure event when the water is untreated.

PFAS Dermal Absorption

PFAS — per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances, known as "forever chemicals" — have been detected in water supplies across the country. The Consumer Reports testing program has found that reverse osmosis systems certified to NSF/ANSI 58 are effective at removing PFAS from drinking water.7 However, PFAS compounds are also absorbed dermally. Whole-house catalytic carbon filtration can reduce PFAS from every tap and every shower, not just the kitchen sink. The SoftPro Chlorine+ filter targets PFAS at the point of entry, protecting the entire household from all three exposure routes simultaneously.

3. Why Under-Sink Filters Can't Protect Showers, Laundry, or Appliances

An under-sink filter is a point-of-use device. It is installed on the cold-water line under the kitchen sink and treats only the water coming out of that single faucet. It does not — and physically cannot — treat water that goes to:

  • Your shower or bath — leaving your skin and lungs exposed to chlorine, chloramine, and THMs every time you bathe.
  • Your washing machine — so chlorine continues to fade colors and degrade fabric fibers.
  • Your dishwasher — meaning chemical deposits and sediment still accumulate on dishes and internal components.
  • Your water heater — untreated water shortens the lifespan of your water heater by up to 50%, according to research by the Water Quality Research Foundation.8

The NSF notes that whole-house systems address "all the water in your home" while POU systems cover only specific areas such as the kitchen faucet.9 If you install an under-sink filter and call it a day, the vast majority of your home's water is still untreated.

4. POE vs. POU: A Simple Framework

Understanding the difference between point-of-entry and point-of-use treatment is the single most important decision you will make about home water filtration.

Point of Entry (POE)Point of Use (POU)
Installation LocationMain water line where water enters the homeAt a single fixture (usually under the kitchen sink)
Water TreatedEvery drop in the entire houseOne faucet only
Protects ShowersYesNo
Protects AppliancesYesNo
Protects LaundryYesNo
Contaminants TargetedChlorine, chloramine, sediment, PFAS, VOCsLead, PFAS, TDS, nitrates (varies by filter)
MaintenanceMedia change every 5+ yearsCartridges every 6–12 months
Upfront Cost Range$600–$1,500$100–$600

Culligan's guide puts it plainly: "A point-of-entry system is installed at the main water line to treat all the water you use in your entire home," while a POU system "treats only a portion of that water."10 WaterCare makes the same distinction, noting that POE systems are "high capacity" and "treat thousands of gallons of water a day" while POU systems serve smaller quantities.11

5. The Combination Approach: POE Carbon + POU RO

The smartest water-treatment strategy isn't either/or — it's both. A whole-house carbon filter at the point of entry handles the broad-stroke contaminants that affect your entire home: chlorine, chloramine, sediment, PFAS, and pesticides. Then a dedicated reverse osmosis system under the kitchen sink polishes your drinking water to the highest standard.

Mid Atlantic Water, with over 30 years in the industry, recommends exactly this approach: "The whole house column wins every row about coverage, and the RO column wins every row about depth. Neither one beats the other at its own job."12

This combination is the industry standard for a reason. Consumer Reports suggests pairing "a basic, less expensive whole-house water filter with an NSF/ANSI-certified point-of-use water filter for the water you ingest."13

8. What to Do: Test, Install, Complete

If you are ready to move from partial protection to true whole-home water quality, here is your three-step plan:

Step 1: Test Your Water

Before buying any filtration equipment, test your water. Your local utility provides an annual Consumer Confidence Report (CCR) that tells you what is in your municipal supply.17 For an even clearer picture, order a home water test kit that screens for chlorine, chloramine, pH, TDS, hardness, iron, lead, and bacteria. The EPA recommends testing for lead specifically if you live in an older home with lead pipes or solder.18

Step 2: Install a POE Filter First

Your highest-impact investment is a whole-house carbon filter installed at the main water line. The SoftPro Chlorine+ is an excellent choice for city water. It treats the 99% of water that touches your skin, hair, appliances, and plumbing. This single installation protects every shower, every load of laundry, every dish cycle, and your entire plumbing system from chemical damage and sediment accumulation.

Step 3: Add a POU RO System at the Kitchen Tap

Once your whole house is protected, add an under-sink reverse osmosis system at the kitchen sink for the water you drink and cook with. The RO membrane removes lead, nitrates, fluoride, arsenic, and remaining dissolved solids to deliver the highest-quality drinking water possible. The combination is unbeatable: POE for broad whole-home protection, POU for final polish at the point of consumption.
