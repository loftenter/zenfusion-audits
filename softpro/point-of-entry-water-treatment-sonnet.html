The 1% Myth: Where Your Water Actually Goes

Here's a statistic that should change how you think about water filtration: you and your family use approximately 1% of household water for drinking and cooking. The remaining 99% flows through showers, baths, washing machines, dishwashers, and toilets.1

An average American household uses 300 gallons of water per day. Of that, roughly 3 gallons go into bodies as drinking water. The other 297 gallons touch skin, wash clothes, clean dishes, and flush away.

That under-sink filter? It treats the 3 gallons. The 297 gallons that contact your body's largest organ—your skin—flow through completely untreated.

The Exposure Routes You're Missing

Water contamination doesn't only matter when you swallow it. Your skin is highly permeable, and the heat and duration of a typical shower create ideal conditions for chemical absorption and inhalation.

Dermal Absorption

Research published by the European Commission's Science Hub demonstrates that PFAS compounds (forever chemicals) are readily absorbed through human skin, particularly during prolonged water contact like showering and bathing.2 A single 10-minute shower can result in dermal and inhalation exposure equivalent to drinking 2 liters of the same contaminated water.3

Chlorine—the most common municipal water treatment chemical—is also absorbed dermally. While drinking chlorinated water exposes you to a controlled dose, showering in it means your entire body surface is absorbing chlorine for 5-15 minutes daily.

Inhalation in Shower Steam

When water heats up in your shower, volatile contaminants evaporate into the air you breathe. Chlorine becomes chlorine gas. Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) like trihalomethanes—cancer-linked disinfection byproducts formed when chlorine reacts with organic matter—vaporize and concentrate in enclosed bathroom air.4

The American Journal of Public Health documented that you can absorb more chlorine and related compounds from a 10-minute shower than from drinking eight glasses of the same water.5

Under-sink filters do nothing to address this exposure. While you carefully filter what goes into your glass, your family is breathing and absorbing the same contaminants every time they shower.

Why Under-Sink Filters Can't Protect Your Household

Point-of-use (POU) filtration—filters installed at a single tap—serves an important purpose, but it's fundamentally limited by physics: water that never passes through the filter never gets filtered.

Here's what an under-sink filter cannot do:

  • Treat shower or bath water — Dermal and inhalation exposure continues unaddressed
  • Protect your laundry — Chlorine and hard minerals continue damaging fabrics and fading colors
  • Extend appliance life — Sediment, chlorine, and minerals continue degrading water heaters, dishwashers, and washing machines
  • Protect secondary taps — Bathroom sinks, utility sinks, and outdoor faucets remain untreated
  • Prevent pipe corrosion — Aggressive water chemistry continues wearing down plumbing throughout the house

Consumer Reports puts it plainly: "If your goal is to have filtered water for drinking and cooking, an under-sink water filter is probably all you need. [But] you probably don't need filtered toilet water" if that's your only concern.6 The publication acknowledges whole-house systems are necessary when water quality affects the entire household—which it does in the majority of American homes dealing with chlorinated municipal water or well water issues.

Point of Entry vs. Point of Use: Understanding the Difference

Feature Point of Entry (Whole House) Point of Use (Under-Sink)
Installation Point Main water line before branching Under one sink (usually kitchen)
Coverage Every tap, shower, appliance One faucet only
Protects From Chlorine, sediment, taste/odor at ALL fixtures Contaminants at ONE tap
Dermal/Inhalation Protection Yes—treats shower water No—shower water untreated
Appliance Protection Yes—extends water heater, dishwasher, washer life No—appliances receive untreated water
Best Use Case Household-wide water issues, chlorine exposure, well water Targeted drinking water purification (lead, PFAS)

Point of Entry (POE) systems install on your main water line immediately after the meter, before water branches to individual fixtures. Every drop entering your home passes through the filter first. This is where whole-house carbon filters, sediment filters, and combination systems live.7

Point of Use (POU) systems install at individual fixtures—most commonly under the kitchen sink. They treat only the water coming from that specific tap. Reverse osmosis (RO) systems are typically POU because they're slow, produce reject water, and would be impractical for a whole-house application.8

The Combination Approach That Actually Works

The most effective residential water treatment strategy isn't POE vs. POU—it's POE plus POU.

Here's why: POE and POU systems solve different problems with different technologies.

What POE Carbon Filtration Does Best

  • Removes chlorine and chloramine throughout the entire home (NSF-certified systems achieve 97%+ reduction9)
  • Reduces disinfection byproducts (trihalomethanes, haloacetic acids)
  • Eliminates taste and odor issues at every tap
  • Removes sediment and particulates before they damage fixtures and appliances
  • Protects skin and hair from chlorine exposure during bathing

What it doesn't do: POE carbon systems don't remove dissolved minerals (hardness), heavy metals to the same certified levels as RO, or contaminants like nitrates and fluoride.

What POU Reverse Osmosis Does Best

  • Removes 90-99% of dissolved solids, including sodium, calcium, and sulfate10
  • Certified removal of lead, arsenic, chromium-6, and other heavy metals
  • Removes PFAS (forever chemicals) to below detectable limits
  • Eliminates nitrates from agricultural runoff
  • Removes fluoride (if that's a concern for your household)

What it doesn't do: RO systems are slow, waste 3-4 gallons per gallon produced, and only treat one tap.

The Strategic Combination

Install POE carbon filtration to handle the 99% of water that touches your body, clothes, and appliances. Then install POU reverse osmosis at your kitchen sink to achieve drinking-water purity for the 1% you ingest.11

This isn't redundant—it's targeted. The POE system handles exposure protection and infrastructure protection. The RO system handles ingestion protection. And because the POE filter removes chlorine before water reaches the RO membrane, your RO system lasts significantly longer.

Example: Mid-Atlantic Water, a 32-year water treatment company, recommends this exact approach for city water customers. Their advice: "Follow your test results. If your water shows lead, PFAS, nitrates, or elevated dissolved solids, buy the under-sink RO first: it protects what you ingest, which is the higher-stakes exposure. If your water tests clean but chlorine is making showers unpleasant and drying out your skin, the whole house carbon filter is the one doing the daily work."12

What You Should Do Right Now

If you're relying only on an under-sink filter, here's the honest path forward:

  1. Test your water. You can't treat what you don't measure. Get a comprehensive water test ($30-$200 depending on parameters) that includes chlorine, lead, PFAS, VOCs, hardness, and pH. If you're on well water, include bacteria, nitrates, and iron.
  2. Install point-of-entry filtration first if your test shows chlorine/chloramine (city water) or broad contaminant issues. This addresses the 99% of water touching your skin and protecting your home's infrastructure.
  3. Add point-of-use RO at your kitchen sink if testing revealed lead, PFAS, nitrates, arsenic, or elevated TDS. This gives you drinking-water purity beyond what POE carbon alone provides.
  4. Replace your under-sink filter with an RO system if you already have POE treatment but want maximum drinking water protection. The carbon filter upstream will extend your RO membrane's life significantly.
  5. Maintain both systems. POE carbon media lasts ~5 years. RO pre/post filters need annual replacement; membranes last 2-3 years. Follow the manufacturer's schedule.

The uncomfortable truth is this: if you're only filtering at the kitchen sink, you're protecting about 1% of your family's water exposure. The other 99% is still carrying chlorine, PFAS, and whatever else your municipality or well delivers—straight into your showers, your washing machine, and ultimately, your body.

Point-of-entry treatment isn't a luxury. When you understand the exposure routes and run the real numbers, it's the only approach that actually protects your household.
