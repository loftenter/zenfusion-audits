What's Actually in Your Tap Water: The Hidden Dangers of Unfiltered Municipal Water

By Craig "The Water Guy" Phillips  ·  September 17, 2026

Municipal water treatment was designed to prevent acute waterborne diseases — and on that score it succeeds. But the protections largely stop there. Aging infrastructure, unregulated chemicals, and the byproducts of treatment itself all slip through. What flows from your tap is legal — but legal doesn't always mean safe.

Key Takeaways

  • An estimated 9.2 million lead service lines still deliver water to American homes — and the EPA's maximum contaminant level goal for lead is zero.
  • The USGS found PFAS "forever chemicals" in 45% of U.S. tap water samples; the EPA's new limit for PFOA and PFOS is just 4 parts per trillion.
  • Disinfection byproducts like trihalomethanes (TTHMs) and haloacetic acids (HAA5s) form when chlorine reacts with organic matter and are linked to cancer and reproductive harm.
  • Chlorine and chloramine vapors in shower steam can irritate skin, aggravate asthma, and enter your bloodstream through inhalation.
  • Pharmaceutical residues, pesticides (atrazine), and nitrate fertilizers routinely survive municipal treatment and end up in your glass.
  • A whole-house catalytic carbon filter plus a point-of-use reverse osmosis system offers the most comprehensive home defense.

1. Lead: The Poison in Your Pipes

There is no safe level of lead in the human body. The EPA's maximum contaminant level goal (MCLG) for lead in drinking water is zero — not 15 parts per billion, which is the "action level," but zero. Lead is a potent neurotoxin that accumulates in bones and tissues over decades. In children, even low-level exposure impairs cognitive development, lowers IQ, and causes behavioral disorders. In adults, it raises blood pressure, damages kidneys, and increases cardiovascular mortality risk [1].

Yet an estimated 9.2 million lead service lines remain in service across the United States — the literal pipes connecting water mains to homes [10]. These pipes are concentrated in older housing stock throughout the Northeast, Midwest, and Great Lakes regions. When water chemistry shifts, protective mineral scales dissolve and lead concentrations spike. Even under normal conditions, levels fluctuate, and no amount is safe. The CDC warns that no blood lead level in children has been identified that does not affect learning or behavior [9].

Municipalities add orthophosphate corrosion inhibitors to control lead release, but these treatments are imperfect — and they do nothing for lead that enters water after it leaves the plant. The only reliable fix is filtration at the point of use.

2. PFAS Forever Chemicals: Everywhere and Indestructible

In 2023, the U.S. Geological Survey tested tap water from 716 locations nationwide. The result: 45% of samples contained one or more PFAS compounds [2]. That is nearly every other tap. PFAS — per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances — are synthetic chemicals used since the 1940s in nonstick cookware, waterproof fabrics, firefighting foams, and food packaging. They are called "forever chemicals" because the carbon-fluorine bond that makes them useful also makes them virtually indestructible in the environment and in the human body.

In April 2024, the EPA issued its first-ever legally enforceable drinking water standards for six PFAS compounds, including a limit of just 4 parts per trillion for PFOA and PFOS — the lowest concentration that modern laboratories can reliably detect [8]. To visualize 4 parts per trillion, imagine four drops of ink in an Olympic-size swimming pool. Epidemiology links PFAS exposure to kidney and testicular cancer, thyroid disruption, immune suppression (including reduced vaccine response in children), elevated cholesterol, and ulcerative colitis. These chemicals bioaccumulate: the half-life of PFOA in the human body is roughly 3.8 years. Every glass of unfiltered tap water consumed from a contaminated source adds to that burden.

3. Disinfection Byproducts: The Irony of Treatment

To kill pathogens, water treatment plants add chlorine or chloramine. That step prevents cholera. But when these disinfectants react with naturally occurring organic matter in source water — decaying leaves, algae, soil humus — they form a class of compounds called disinfection byproducts (DBPs). The two most studied groups are total trihalomethanes (TTHMs) and haloacetic acids (HAA5s), both regulated by the EPA [1]. The regulation exists because the evidence of harm is overwhelming: long-term exposure is associated with bladder cancer, colorectal cancer, and adverse reproductive outcomes including low birth weight and miscarriage.

More than 260 million Americans receive chlorinated drinking water. A meta-analysis in the Journal of the National Cancer Institute found a statistically significant association between chlorinated water and bladder cancer risk, with the attributable fraction estimated at 14 to 16 percent in populations with lifelong exposure. And here is the catch: boiling water reduces chlorine, but it concentrates non-volatile DBPs as water evaporates. The solution is removal before ingestion.

4. Chlorine & Chloramine: What You Can't See in Steam

Most people think of chlorine as a swimming-pool smell — unpleasant but harmless at low concentrations. The reality is more complicated. When you take a hot shower, chlorine and chloramine volatilize into steam. Inhaled chloroform and related compounds enter the bloodstream directly through the lungs, bypassing the liver's detoxification pathways — which means inhalation exposure can be more harmful than ingestion for certain compounds. A study in the American Journal of Public Health found that chloroform blood levels rose significantly after a 10-minute shower and were consistently higher than levels measured after drinking a liter of the same water.

Dermatologists also point to chlorine as a contributor to chronic dry skin, eczema flares, and brittle hair — it strips the natural protective oils from skin and scalp. For people with asthma, chloramine vapors can trigger bronchial constriction. Whole-house dechlorination addresses the inhalation and dermal routes that a pitcher filter cannot touch.

5. Pharmaceuticals: An Unregulated Frontier

When you take a pill, your body metabolizes only a fraction. The rest is excreted and flushed into the wastewater system — and conventional treatment plants were never designed to remove dissolved pharmaceuticals. A 2008 Associated Press investigation found trace levels of antibiotics, antidepressants, anticonvulsants, and sex hormones in the drinking water supplies of 41 million Americans. With an aging population and rising prescription drug use, the situation has likely worsened since.

Concentrations are measured in parts per trillion or even parts per quadrillion — far below therapeutic doses. The concern lies in chronic, low-dose exposure across decades and the effects of chemical mixtures. Endocrine-disrupting compounds like ethinyl estradiol can affect fish reproduction at one part per trillion; what they do to humans over a lifetime is poorly studied. The EPA does not currently regulate any pharmaceutical compounds in drinking water. Advanced filtration — activated carbon and reverse osmosis — are the only reliable removal methods.

6. Agricultural Runoff: Pesticides and Nitrates

Atrazine, the second most widely used herbicide in the U.S., is banned in the European Union for its endocrine-disrupting effects, yet it is detected in roughly 94% of drinking water samples from agricultural watersheds. The EPA's legal limit of 3 parts per billion is over 30 times higher than what recent research associates with preterm birth and low birth weight [1, 6].

Nitrates, primarily from fertilizer runoff and concentrated animal feeding operations, are another persistent threat. The EPA sets the maximum contaminant level at 10 milligrams per liter because above that threshold, nitrates interfere with the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity, causing "blue baby syndrome" (methemoglobinemia) in infants. Even below the regulatory limit, long-term nitrate exposure has been linked to thyroid disease, colorectal cancer, and neural tube defects. Municipal plants can reduce nitrates only if equipped with ion-exchange or reverse osmosis infrastructure — and many are not. The Environmental Working Group's Tap Water Database documents nearly 2,400 water systems across 49 states serving 7 million people with nitrate above what EWG scientists consider safe [10].

8. What You Can Do About It

The picture is grim, but the solution is straightforward and has never been more accessible. Three steps cover 99% of households:

Step 1: Test Your Water

You cannot fix a problem you have not measured. Start with your utility's annual Consumer Confidence Report (CCR), mailed or posted online by July 1 each year. Then supplement it with an independent laboratory test — the Environmental Working Group's Tap Water Database can help you identify what to test for based on your ZIP code [10]. A comprehensive panel covering lead, copper, nitrates, arsenic, PFAS, VOCs, bacteria, and disinfection byproducts typically costs $150 to $300. You are not guessing anymore; you are engineering a solution to a known problem.

Step 2: Whole-House Catalytic Carbon Filtration

A high-quality whole-house catalytic carbon filter is the workhorse of home water treatment. Unlike standard activated carbon, catalytic carbon is processed at high temperatures to restructure its pore surface, dramatically increasing its capacity to adsorb chloramine and hydrogen sulfide. The SoftPro Chlorine+ upflow catalytic carbon system is purpose-built for this job: it removes chlorine, chloramine, disinfection byproducts, volatile organic compounds, pesticides, herbicides, and improves taste and odor at every tap in the house — including the shower, where inhalation exposure matters most [3, 4]. Because it is an upflow design, it wastes zero water to backwashing, requires no electricity, and delivers treated water throughout the home with negligible pressure drop.

Step 3: Point-of-Use Reverse Osmosis for Drinking Water

A whole-house carbon filter removes the bulk of organic contaminants and disinfectants, but for the highest-risk dissolved contaminants — lead, PFAS, nitrates, arsenic, chromium-6 — you want a point-of-use reverse osmosis system installed under your kitchen sink. Reverse osmosis forces water through a semipermeable membrane with pores so fine they reject ions as small as sodium and chloride, effectively removing 95 to 99 percent of dissolved contaminants. Paired with a catalytic carbon pre-filter and a remineralization stage, it produces the cleanest, best-tasting drinking water available in any home. The combination of whole-house catalytic carbon plus a dedicated RO at the kitchen sink is the gold standard in residential water treatment — comprehensive, field-proven, and dramatically more cost-effective than bottled water delivery.

Municipal water treatment remains one of the great public health achievements of the twentieth century. But the job it was asked to do a century ago — stop waterborne disease — is not the job we need it to do today. Lead pipes that should have been replaced decades ago, unregulated PFAS compounds that outlast every structure ever built, and pharmaceutical residues from a medicated population have all outstripped the treatment plants built to handle them. The burden has shifted to the homeowner.

Fortunately, the tools to meet that burden exist and have never been more effective. Test your water. Filter it at the point of entry and the point of use. And sleep better knowing you have closed the gap between what's legal and what's safe.
